Breaking the current of a heater and that of a starting motor is not the same work for the contacts. The standard **IEC 60947-4-1** therefore classifies uses into **categories**.

![Utilisation categories: AC-1 slightly inductive loads, AC-3 cage motors switched off while running, AC-4 reversing and inching, DC-1 and DC-3 on DC](/static/content/moteurs/en/m7/categories.svg)

- **AC-1**: slightly inductive loads (heating, incandescent lighting). The current broken is the operating current; the arc is weak.
- **AC-3**: cage motors, started then **switched off while running**. When making, the contactor withstands the starting current (6 to 8 In); when breaking, the motor is turning and draws only its rated current: breaking is easy.
- **AC-4**: cage motors **switched off during starting**: reversing, inching, plugging. The contactor then breaks the starting current itself.
- **DC-1, DC-3**: their DC equivalents, where the arc is harder to extinguish.

## Why it matters

![Current to break: about In in AC-3, about 6 In in AC-4](/static/content/moteurs/en/m7/coupure.svg)

In AC-4, the contactor breaks about **6 times more current** than in AC-3: the arc is much more energetic, the contacts wear much faster. The same contactor can make a million operations in AC-3, but only a few tens of thousands in AC-4. So a contactor is chosen **in the category of its real use**: for a motor often reversed or inched, oversize it.

## Key points

- AC-1 resistive, AC-3 motor switched off running, AC-4 reversing and inching.
- AC-4 breaks about 6 In: much faster wear.
- A contactor is chosen in the category of its use.
