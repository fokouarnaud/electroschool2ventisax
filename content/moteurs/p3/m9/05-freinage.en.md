A motor that is switched off coasts to a stop, sometimes over tens of seconds. To stop it quickly, or to **hold** the load at standstill, it is braked.

## Electrical braking

- **Plugging**: two phases are swapped while the motor is still turning; the field then rotates the other way and brakes very hard. A zero-speed switch cuts the supply just at standstill, otherwise the motor would start the other way. Very high current, contactors in AC-4.
- **DC injection**: the motor is disconnected from the supply, then **direct** current is sent into two phases. The stator then creates a **fixed** field; the rotor turning in it is braked, with no mechanical wear.

![Injection braking: motor switched off, DC injection, fixed field, rotor braked](/static/content/moteurs/en/m9/injection.svg)

Electrical braking stops the motor, but **does not hold** it at standstill: a suspended load would come down.

## The electromechanical brake

To hold a load (lifting, travel, a machine that must not turn at standstill), a **spring-applied, power-off brake** is fitted on the shaft: **springs** clamp a disc; a **coil**, energised at the same time as the motor, releases the brake.

![Spring-applied electromechanical brake: the springs clamp the disc, the energised coil releases it; without current, the brake applies](/static/content/moteurs/en/m9/frein.svg)

Its principle is a safety rule: **without current, the brake applies**. A power cut or a broken wire stops and holds the load, instead of letting it fall. Its linings wear: the air gap and braking torque are checked regularly.

## Key points

- Plugging: powerful, zero-speed cut-off mandatory.
- DC injection: fixed field, no wear, no holding.
- Spring-applied brake: without current, it applies.
