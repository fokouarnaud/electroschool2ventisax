A DC motor has a fixed part and a rotating part.

![Cross-section of a DC motor: frame, field poles, rotating armature, commutator and brushes in the centre](/static/content/moteurs/en/m5/coupe.svg)

- The **field** (on the stator, the fixed part) creates the magnetic field: wound poles or permanent magnets, fixed to the **frame**.
- The **armature** (on the rotor, the rotating part) carries the windings through which the current flows; it is subject to the forces and drives the shaft.
- The **commutator** is a cylinder made of copper **segments** insulated from one another, each connected to an armature coil.
- The carbon **brushes** are fixed; they rub on the commutator and bring the current to it.

## The commutator's role

As it turns, the commutator presents different segments to the brushes: the current thus reverses in each coil at the exact moment it passes from one pole to the other. The torque always keeps the same direction: this is **mechanical commutation**.

![Segmented commutator and carbon brushes: the fixed brushes rub on the segments and reverse the current in each coil at the right moment](/static/content/moteurs/en/m5/collecteur.svg)

## The weak point

Commutator and brushes wear by friction and produce small sparks. Brushes are checked and replaced regularly; a scored or blackened commutator is cleaned or skimmed. This is the main maintenance task on DC motors, and the reason AC motors are often preferred.

## Key points

- Field on the stator, armature on the rotor.
- Commutator + brushes: the current reverses in each coil at the right moment.
- Brushes wear: check and replace them.
