Two variants of the three-phase motor meet particular needs.

## The wound-rotor motor

Its rotor carries a real three-phase winding, connected to three **slip rings** on which brushes rub. **Resistors** can thus be inserted in the rotor circuit.

![Wound-rotor motor: three slip rings connect the rotor to starting resistors, removed in steps](/static/content/moteurs/en/m6/rotor-bobine.svg)

At start-up, the resistors limit the current **and raise the torque**: ideal for starting a heavy load (crusher, overhead crane, mill). They are removed in steps as the motor gathers speed; in normal running, the rotor is short-circuited and the motor behaves like a cage motor. Rings and brushes need maintenance: today, a variable-speed drive often replaces this solution.

## The synchronous motor

Its rotor carries **permanent magnets** or a winding fed with direct current: it is dragged by the rotating field **exactly at its speed**. It has no slip.

![Speed versus load: the synchronous motor stays at 1,500 rpm, the induction motor slows slightly](/static/content/moteurs/en/m6/synchrone.svg)

Its speed depends only on the frequency and the number of poles, whatever the load (up to a limit where it "pulls out"). It does not start by itself on the supply: it is brought up by a drive or by an auxiliary cage. It equips precise-speed drives, large compressors and, as the **permanent-magnet motor**, electric vehicles and servomotors: its efficiency is very high.

## Key points

- Wound rotor: slip rings + resistors, high starting torque.
- Synchronous: speed = ns, no slip.
- Permanent-magnet synchronous: high efficiency, started by a drive.
