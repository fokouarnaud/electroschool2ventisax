A bolted short circuit, a switching mistake or a tool falling between two busbars can strike an **arc flash**: current crosses the ionised air between two conductors. In a fraction of a second the arc releases intense heat, blinding light, a pressure wave and molten metal. The burns it causes happen **without touching** any conductor.

![Arc flash between two live conductors; beyond the arc flash boundary, the energy received becomes bearable](/static/content/moteurs/en/m1/arc.svg)

## The arc flash boundary

The closer you are to the arc, the more energy you receive. An **arc flash boundary** is defined: the distance within which an unprotected person risks second-degree burns. It depends on the available short-circuit current and on how fast the protection trips: a breaker that opens quickly reduces the arc energy. In an industrial distribution cabinet, this boundary can exceed one metre.

## Personal protective equipment

When work near exposed live parts cannot be avoided (measurement, diagnosis), personal protective equipment (PPE) suited to the level of risk is worn:

![Protective equipment: arc-rated helmet and face shield, flame-resistant clothing, insulating gloves, insulating shoes, insulating mat](/static/content/moteurs/en/m1/epi.svg)

- arc-rated **helmet and face shield**, against light and projections;
- **flame-resistant clothing**: synthetic clothing melts and sticks to the skin, making the burn worse;
- **insulating gloves** of the class suited to the voltage, checked before each use (inflation test, no cuts);
- insulating **shoes** and **mat**, which break the path to the ground.

PPE is the **last line of defence**, not the first solution. First aim to work de-energised (lockout, section 5), then move away or cover exposed parts, and only then put on the equipment.

## Key points

- An arc burns at a distance, without contact.
- Fast tripping reduces the arc energy and the arc flash boundary.
- PPE comes after de-energising and collective protection.
