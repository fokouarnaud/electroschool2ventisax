Between good conductors (copper) and insulators (plastic), there are in-between materials: **semiconductors**, the most common being silicon. Pure silicon conducts very poorly. But it can be "doped" by adding a tiny amount of another element:

- **N** doping brings free electrons, which carry negative charge;
- **P** doping creates "holes", empty places that behave like positive charges.

## The PN junction

A diode is a piece of silicon with one half doped P and the other N. Where they meet, electrons from the N region fill holes in the P region. A thin **depletion zone** forms, emptied of free carriers, which acts as a barrier.

![P region with its holes, N region with its electrons, and the depletion zone between them](/static/content/electro/en/m2/jonction.svg)

To cross this barrier, a minimum voltage must be applied in the right direction: about **0.6 to 0.7 V** for silicon. The other way round, the barrier widens and almost no current flows.

## Symbol and package

The diode symbol is an arrow that shows the **forward direction** of current, from the anode (A, P side) to the cathode (K, N side). The vertical bar stands for the cathode. On the part itself, the cathode is marked by a painted **band**.

![Diode symbol from anode to cathode, and package with the band on the cathode side](/static/content/electro/en/m2/symbole.svg)

A diode fitted the wrong way round is the most common wiring mistake: get into the habit of looking for the band before soldering.

## Key points

- N doping: free electrons; P doping: holes.
- The PN junction lets current through in one direction only, from anode to cathode.
- The band on the package marks the cathode.
