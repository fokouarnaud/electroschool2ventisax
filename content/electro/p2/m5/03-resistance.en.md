A resistor responds to AC exactly as it does to DC: at every instant, the current follows the voltage according to Ohm's law.

![AC source of 10 V RMS, ammeter and 1 kΩ resistor: the current is 10 mA RMS](/static/content/electro/en/m5/r-circuit.svg)

## Ohm's law with RMS values

Apply Part 1's Ohm's law to the **RMS values**:

**Irms = Urms / R**

With 10 V RMS across 1 kΩ: Irms = 10 / 1,000 = **10 mA** RMS. A multimeter on mA~ placed in series shows that value. The same calculations also work with peak values (Imax = Umax / R), as long as you do not mix the two.

## Voltage and current in phase

In a resistor, current and voltage reach their maximum at the same moment and go through zero together: they are said to be **in phase**.

![Voltage u and current i in a resistor: both curves rise, fall and cross zero together](/static/content/electro/en/m5/r-phase.svg)

## Power

The average power dissipated is also calculated with RMS values:

**P = Urms × Irms = Urms² / R**

With 10 V RMS across 1 kΩ: P = 100 / 1,000 = **0.1 W**. That is the power to compare with the resistor's rating (0.25 W for a common resistor).

## Key points

- For a resistor, Ohm's law still holds with RMS values.
- u and i are in phase: zero phase shift.
- P = Urms × Irms.
