In Part 1, a battery's voltage stayed constant: that is **direct current**. A mains socket, a car alternator or the output of a signal generator instead supply a voltage that keeps changing direction: that is **alternating current**. Its most common shape is the **sine wave**.

![A 50 Hz sine wave: it rises to Umax = 10 V, falls to −10 V and repeats every 20 ms](/static/content/electro/en/m5/sinusoide.svg)

## Period and frequency

A sine wave repeats itself exactly. The time taken by one complete cycle is the **period** T, in seconds. The number of cycles per second is the **frequency** f, in hertz (Hz).

**f = 1 / T**   and   **T = 1 / f**

Mains power in Central Africa and in Europe runs at **50 Hz**: T = 1 / 50 = 0.02 s, that is **20 ms**. A 1 kHz signal has a period of 1 ms.

![Two sine waves over the same time: the 100 Hz one completes two cycles while the 50 Hz one completes only one](/static/content/electro/en/m5/frequences.svg)

## Reading a sine wave

- For half a period the voltage is positive; for the other half it is negative: current flows one way, then the other.
- The voltage goes **through zero twice** in every period.
- The highest value is called the **amplitude** or peak value, written Umax.

A higher frequency means a shorter period: the cycles bunch up on the screen.

## Key points

- AC: the voltage changes direction; the sine wave is its basic shape.
- f = 1 / T; f in hertz, T in seconds.
- 50 Hz means a period of 20 ms.
